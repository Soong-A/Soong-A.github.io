Reworking Res2Net for 3D

发表于2025-11-12|更新于2026-10-11|English Notes
|浏览量:

9 min read

中文版：MM-UNet 编码器：3D Res2Net 的改造

Notes on MM-UNet’s encoder. The paper adapts Res2Net — a network designed for ordinary 2D photographs — into a 3D medical image encoder, while keeping the representations it learned on natural images.

Why Res2Net

Each Res2Net residual block contains hierarchical residual connections inside it, which lets the network use multi-scale information at a granular level and widens the receptive field of every layer.

The analogy that stuck: it is like working with several magnifiers at once.

  • A small magnifier for fine texture and edges — small-scale features.
  • A medium one for local structure — mid-scale features.
  • A large one for the overall outline — large-scale features.

For prostate images, where the boundary is genuinely blurred, having all three at once matters.

How the encoder runs

flowchart TD
  A[3D MR volume] --> B[Convolution stem: parallel 3x3x3 and 1x1x1]
  B --> C[Sum the two paths]
  C --> D[Res2Net block one]
  D --> E[Downsample: stride 2 convolution]
  E --> F[Res2Net block two]
  F --> G[Downsample]
  G --> H[Res2Net block three]
  H --> I[Downsample]
  I --> J[Res2Net block four]
  J --> K[Four feature maps at four scales]

The stem. A 3×3×3 convolution captures basic local features — is this point an edge? — in parallel with a 1×1×1 convolution that mainly adjusts the channel count. Their sum is a feature map rich in basic information.

The four blocks. Each block reduces spatial size and increases abstraction, producing four feature maps that run from fine detail to broad summary. Those four scales are what the decoder later fuses.

Turning 2D into 3D

Two conversions do the work:

The first layer. The 7×7 2D convolution becomes a 7×7×3 3D convolution. A 7×7×3 kernel over a single-channel 3D input is equivalent to a 7×7 kernel over a three-channel 2D input — which is exactly the shape the pretrained weights have.

Every other layer. An x×x 2D convolution becomes an x×x×1 3D convolution.

Why pretraining matters here

The encoder is initialised from a pretrained Res2Net, which transfers representational ability learned on a large natural-image dataset.

Concretely: that network already graduated from ImageNet, where it learned to recognise edges, shapes and textures. Rather than starting from nothing, the model brings that groundwork to prostate MR — like a student who already knows how to read pictures being taught to read medical images. Faster to train, and better at the end.

Channel growth, and the trade it represents

A channel is best understood as one learned “way of describing” the image.

  • At the input, a greyscale medical image has 1 channel: raw intensity.
  • Deep in the network, one channel may respond to edges, another to a particular texture, a third to arc-like structure — possibly the prostate outline.

More channels means more descriptive angles available to the model.

Why increase them

The principle is trading breadth of information (channels) against depth of information (spatial size).

Stage Spatial size Channels What happened
Input 128 × 128 × 128 1 Raw volume, intensity only
After block 1 64 × 64 × 64 64 Downsampled; 64 basic features now describe the smaller map
After block 2 32 × 32 × 32 128 Halved again; channels doubled to compensate
After block 3 16 × 16 × 16 256 Halved again; channels doubled again
After block 4 8 × 8 × 8 512 Least spatial information, 512 highly abstract concepts

Downsampling sacrifices precise localisation. Increasing the channel count compensates: each position is described more coarsely in space but by far more kinds of feature, so the information content is preserved or even increased.

Where the increase actually happens

Not inside the Res2Net block. Channels grow in the transition between blocks:

  1. Features enter a Res2Net block with, say, 64 channels.
  2. Inside the block they are split and processed, but the output channel count usually stays the same.
  3. Before the next block, a stride-2 3D convolution halves the spatial size and doubles the channels to 128.

Why the stem exists at all

Think of it as a production line.

Station one — the stem (3×3×3 + 1×1×1). A primary processing shop. It takes raw material — pixels — and does the most basic, most general work: finding edges, corners and blobs, then tidying them into organised components.

Station two onward — Res2Net blocks. A precision assembly shop. It takes those components and performs complex multi-scale analysis, combining basic features into richer patterns.

Could Res2Net process raw pixels directly? Technically yes, but badly:

  1. Inefficient. Res2Net blocks are structurally complex and expensive; spending them on trivial edge detection is wasteful.
  2. Harder to optimise. Asking one module to learn everything from the lowest to the highest level makes training unstable.
  3. No division of labour. The stem specialises in low-level extraction; Res2Net specialises in mid- and high-level fusion.

They are not doing the same job twice — they are consecutive stations. The stem gives Res2Net better material to work with, which is what lets it perform.
