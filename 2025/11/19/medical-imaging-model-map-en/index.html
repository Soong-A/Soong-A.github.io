A map of medical imaging models

发表于2025-11-19|更新于2026-10-11|English Notes
|浏览量:

14 min read

中文版：医学影像模型全景：从骨干网络到基础模型

An attempt to organise the model landscape in medical image analysis into something I could navigate, rather than a list of names I half-recognised.

1. Backbones — the building blocks

These are the bricks that more complex models are made from.

Model Core idea Role in medical imaging
CNN Convolution extracts local features; pooling reduces dimension The starting point for everything. Extracts basic features such as edges and texture
VGG Depth through stacked small kernels Simple structure; often the encoder backbone for U-Net and similar
ResNet Residual connections solve vanishing gradients in deep networks The most common backbone. Strong features for classification, segmentation and detection
DenseNet Every layer connects to all previous layers; very high feature reuse Efficient with limited data, which suits many medical tasks
EfficientNet Compound scaling of depth, width and resolution Good where compute is constrained; light and efficient
Vision Transformer Patch the image, use self-attention for global dependency Fixes the CNN’s limited receptive field; suits tasks needing global context

2. Task-specific models

Segmentation

Model Core idea Why it matters here
U-Net Encoder–decoder with skip connections The gold standard for medical segmentation. Symmetric, elegant, precise where structures are complex
U-Net++ Dense skip connections between encoder and decoder Better accuracy than U-Net, especially on complex boundaries
Attention U-Net Attention gates on the skip connections Good for small targets in cluttered backgrounds; reduces false positives
V-Net U-Net extended to 3D, introduces Dice loss Built for 3D volumes such as CT and MRI
nnU-Net A framework, not a network — auto-configures the whole pipeline The benchmark that keeps winning challenges. Proof that a robust pipeline beats a clever architecture
SETR / Swin-Unet Pure or hierarchical Transformer segmentation Explores long-range dependency for large structures

Classification and detection

Model Core idea Use
EfficientNet / ResNet Feature extractor plus a dense head Image-level classification, e.g. pneumonia on a chest X-ray
Faster R-CNN Two-stage: propose regions, then classify and refine High-precision lesion localisation
YOLO / SSD Single-stage regression, very fast Real-time screening or surgical navigation
DETR Transformer treats detection as set prediction Removes anchors and NMS; simplifies the pipeline

Generative and self-supervised

Model Core idea Use
GAN Generator and discriminator trained adversarially Data augmentation for rare cases, modality synthesis, denoising, super-resolution
Diffusion models Learn the distribution through noising and denoising Richer detail than GANs; generation, reconstruction, anomaly detection
MAE / SimCLR Self-supervised pretext tasks on unlabelled data Addresses the cost of medical annotation — pretrain on unlabelled scans, fine-tune on few labels

3. Foundation models

Type Core idea Examples Why it matters
General vision Trained on huge general image–text pairs CLIP Zero-shot classification via prompting, but a real domain gap in medicine
Domain-specific Pretrained on large multimodal medical data — images plus reports Med-PaLM M, LVM Genuinely built for the domain; generalises across classification, segmentation, detection and QA

How to actually learn this

flowchart TD
  A[Start with U-Net and ResNet] --> B[Understand the code, not just the diagram]
  B --> C[Then study nnU-Net]
  C --> D[Learn pipeline design, not just architecture]
  D --> E[Choose by task]
  E --> F[Fine segmentation: Attention U-Net or U-Net++]
  E --> G[3D volumes: V-Net or 3D nnU-Net]
  E --> H[Limited compute: EfficientNet backbone]
  E --> I[Little data: self-supervised or a pretrained foundation model]
  E --> J[Needs global context: ViT or Swin]

The nnU-Net point is the one that changed how I think: it proposes no new architecture at all, and still wins. Robust engineering of preprocessing, topology, training strategy and postprocessing turned out to matter more than a novel module.

Timeline

Before 2012 — before deep learning

Classical machine learning dominated: hand-designed features such as SIFT and HOG, feeding a classifier such as an SVM. In medical imaging this meant registration, level-set or graph-cut segmentation, and basic classification — all hitting a ceiling.

2012–2014 — CNNs arrive

  • AlexNet (2012) — the first decisive win for deep CNNs in image recognition. Researchers immediately began transferring the idea to medical tasks.
  • ZFNet (2013) and VGGNet (2014) — visualisation deepened understanding of what CNNs do; depth was shown to matter. VGG’s regular structure became the backbone for many early medical studies.

2015–2016 — depth and structure

  • U-Net (2015) — the landmark for medical segmentation. Encoder–decoder plus skip connections solved pixel-level localisation. Still the baseline today.
  • ResNet (2015) — residual connections made very deep networks trainable, and replaced VGG as the standard backbone.
  • DenseNet (2016) — pushed the idea further with dense connectivity, improving feature reuse and cutting parameters. Popular where data is scarce.

2017–2019 — attention and efficiency

  • SE-Net (2017) — channel attention: which channels matter.
  • CBAM (2018) — channel and spatial attention together.
  • EfficientNet (2019) — compound scaling for the best accuracy at a given compute budget.

Attention blocks became plug-in upgrades for existing backbones, particularly useful for focusing on small lesions.

2020–2021 — the Transformer arrives

  • ViT (2020) — pure Transformers work for vision, given enough pretraining data.
  • Swin Transformer (2021) — shifted windows and hierarchical design brought multi-scale efficiency, and it quickly became a general-purpose backbone.
  • UNETR / TransUNet (2021) — hybrid CNN–Transformer segmentation; state of the art on several public datasets and U-Net’s most serious competition.

2022 onwards — specialisation, automation, generation

  • nnU-Net — automated configuration proved that pipeline robustness rivals architectural novelty.
  • ConvNeXt (2022) — a “modernised” CNN borrowing Transformer design ideas, overtaking Swin. A reminder that CNNs were not finished.
  • Diffusion models (2022–) — augmentation, reconstruction from low-dose CT, and anomaly detection.

What the map is for

Writing this did not make me an expert on any single row. What it gave me was the ability to place a new paper: when MM-UNet appeared, I could immediately see it as a U-Net descendant that swaps the Transformer bottleneck for Mamba, for exactly the efficiency reason the 2020–2021 row created.
